01

Problem

The head of pharmacy at a public hospital in Lima, the decision maker on the hospital side, receives drug-safety and medical-device-safety notifications on paper and used to copy every field by hand into a yearly Excel file. That copying was the workload, and the same records feed statistics for management, the pharmacotherapy committee and, once a year, DIGEMID, the national medicines authority.

The setting ruled out the easy answers. The hospital PC allows no installs without IT, no USB sticks and no paid AI. Under Peru’s Ley 29733 patients appear as initials only, and a scan with a crossed-out name is still personal data. The pharmacy’s existing workbook is the format other offices expect.

I was the product owner of this personal build: I set the product, brought the pharmacy head’s requests from our calls into the plan and decided what to ship. Claude Code and Codex designed the mechanisms and wrote the code on my brief.

02

The system

Paper goes in at one end and the pharmacy’s own workbook comes out at the other. In between, a model may propose and only a person decides. The limits in the last row shaped every part above them.

03

Key decision

The call
Keep the reader on the hospital PC and let it only propose: the user confirms every field, and there is no button to confirm them all.
Alternatives
  1. A newer cloud model. A 2026 study put the best of them at about 85% on fixed fields.
  2. A confirm-all button to make registering faster.
Why
Privacy law rules out sending a scan abroad and the PC has no paid AI, so the model had to be small and local. A small model does not only miss handwriting, it invents plausible medicine names and lot numbers, and in drug safety an invention is worse than a blank. The reader cleared its 70% target at 74.4%, saves typing and still cannot be trusted with a record, so every proposal waits for a person.
04

What I built

The product is a local web app with two modules, drug safety and device safety, on one page. A notification opens with the scan, straightened automatically, beside a form that follows the official paper. A table with Excel-like filters, a dashboard of 18 cards and each module’s workbook cover reporting.

The rules I set are tagged in the project record with my words and the date, such as «no quiero esconder nada» (“I don’t want to hide anything”) on 27 September, and change only on request. The user’s calls become decisions the same way: after my call with the pharmacy head on 30 October I added a device-safety workbook and hid three device-safety fields the user no longer needed, keeping their columns so that no real value is erased.

The reader's rules

rules I set

  • A proposal is not data

    When
    The reader fills a field
    Then
    It shows in amber and waits for the user

    Correcting a field does not confirm it.

  • Unconfirmed never saves

    When
    The user saves with fields unconfirmed
    Then
    Each one returns to its previous value

  • No confirm-all

    Then
    Every proposal is accepted one at a time

    A button that accepts everything at once was left out on purpose. Small models invent plausible values, so review is field by field.

  • Names become initials

    When
    The reader returns a person's name
    Then
    It arrives as initials, and an unreadable value leaves the field empty
05

AI design choices

  • Where the model runs. On the hospital PC: Qwen3.5-4B, a 4-billion-parameter model, on a llama.cpp engine bundled with the app, about 3.4 GB, with no network. Four local models were compared by size and license.
  • What it may decide. Nothing on its own. It proposes field values from the scan, and a proposal is not data until the user confirms it. The screens do not say AI: the button reads «leer el escaneo», “read the scan”.
  • How quality is measured. A rubric scores the reader field by field against three filled forms I copied by hand, and weighs each field by what it costs to read well. The chart shows where it earns its points.
  • The human in the loop. Checkboxes, dates and codes are read well enough to save typing. Doctors’ handwriting is harder: names and free text score under the target, and a small model can fill a gap with a plausible invention. So the user checks every field against the paper. Judging harm stays with people too: counts describe reporting load, not harm, and the product has no risk score.

How the reader is measured

  • A weighted rubric

    blank 1 · checkbox 1 · short datum 2code 3 · short text 3 · long text 6score = points earned ÷ 276 possiblepass mark 70%

    Each field weighs what it costs to read well. The reference is three filled forms I copied by hand.

  • An invention scores zero

    When
    The model fills a box that is empty on paper
    Then
    It earns nothing

    It filled 11 of 63 empty boxes.

  • A blank form must come out blank

    When
    A new version of the instructions is tried
    If
    A form with nothing filled in is read
    Then
    Every field must come back empty

    Shorter instructions scored the same and wrote two fields into a blank form, so the longer ones stayed.

  • The ceiling is the model, not the prompt

    14 set-ups: 66.5% to 74.6%best of the 14, field by field: 84.0%

    The model errs on the same fields each time, so a vote over several readings does not help.

06

Outcome

The drug-safety export matches the pharmacy’s workbook with 0 differences in every cell, style, width and chart, checked on synthetic records. The reader scores 74.4% against its 70% target, and still nothing it reads becomes data without the user. The update is packaged for the pharmacy’s Windows PC as a portable folder; handover is the next step.

07

Learnings

Keep the agents’ context lean from the first week. By October their working context had grown into a verbatim history so heavy that Codex truncated the instruction file, and the agents kept rebuilding screen rounds I had already replaced. I approved a rewrite into lean topic files: current rules with an owner and date beat a transcript.
