01

Problem

A licensed cannabis retailer ran its shops and its delivery on three business platforms and wanted one product: the register, inventory, compliance, dispatch, a driver app and online ordering. Delivery worked on what I came to call the ice-cream-truck model. Vehicles leave in the morning already stocked and sell from that stock all day, so what a customer can order depends on where they are, which driver is near and what that driver still has on board.

Around that sits the law. Every unit is tracked in Metrc, the state’s track-and-trace system, from the distributor’s manifest to the sale, and the platform’s numbers have to agree with it at every step.

I was the product manager at Valere, working with the client’s team and our technical PM. The client kept final approval of every business rule.

02

The system

One product had to cover both sides of the counter, and each part reads or writes stock that may be in one of three places. The delivery rules, my part of the design, live where the catalog, the checkout and dispatch meet.

03

Discovery

Discovery ran for about two months. I consolidated the requirements of the three platforms into one set, mapped the point-of-sale, inventory, compliance and delivery scope, and drew low-fidelity flows before taking anything to the client, design or engineering, to catch blockers and dependencies early. I had not worked in this domain before. What made it hard showed up at requirement level:

  • Every unit answers to a state ledger. A sale may not push a Metrc quantity below zero, and stock can become unsellable while it sits in a cart.
  • Stock sits in three places. Vault, shop floor and vehicle, each a recorded location, across more than one shop.
  • Two channels compete for one unit. A walk-in customer at the register and an online order can claim the same item in the same second.
  • The driver moves. A driver who is near while the customer browses may be gone, or full, by checkout.
  • Tax and limits are conditional. Tax is charged on tax in a fixed order, and what a customer may buy depends on who they are.
04

Key decision

The call
Offer the customer any driver who is truly near, within three limits, not only the driver assigned to their area.
Alternatives
Strict areas: a customer sees only the stock of the driver assigned to where they live.
Why
A customer just outside one area can have another driver two minutes away, and strict areas would hide that driver. Three limits keep the flexible version workable: how near the driver is, how far they may drift from their own area, and how many orders they already hold. The same limits run again when the order is placed, because the driver keeps moving while the customer shops.
05

What I specified

I designed the delivery logic over days of reasoning and research, then showed it to the client, who liked it, and to our technical PM, whose questions made me refine it. I wrote it as stories with acceptance criteria, error paths included, alongside the rest of the consolidated requirements.

The delivery rules

my design

Written as requirements with acceptance criteria. Every distance, time and cap is a setting the client can tune, and each gate can be switched off for testing.

  • Which stock can this address see?

    When
    A customer enters a delivery address
    If
    The address is in a store-served area
    Then
    The full menu, read from the shop's ledger
    Else
    In a roaming driver's area, the express menu from the kits of drivers inside it. In neither, pickup or another address.

  • Three gates before a driver's stock shows

    When
    The express menu is built
    If
    The driver is within the set minutes or miles of the customer
    And
    The driver has not drifted past the tether from their own area, or the customer is on the way back
    And
    The driver's order queue is under the cap
    Then
    That driver's stock is offered
    Else
    It stays hidden

  • Never show the last unit

    When
    A driver's kit is shown to customers
    Then
    The last unit of each product is held back

    Two customers cannot both order a driver's last item.

  • Check again at the moment of ordering

    When
    The customer presses Place order
    If
    The tether, the cap and all three gates still pass
    And
    No register sale has taken the units
    Then
    The order is created
    Else
    It is blocked with a set message, and the cart shows the items flagged or removed

  • A wrong item is not a short order

    When
    The driver packs an express order
    If
    A scanned item is not on the order
    Then
    Packing stops until the driver clears the warning
    Else
    A known short order goes ahead only with a manager's code

    One is a slip by the person packing, the other a business exception. Each gets its own remedy.

  • A slot has a cap

    When
    A customer picks a delivery slot
    If
    The slot is under its cap
    Then
    The slot can be chosen
    Else
    A later slot or the next day, which makes it a scheduled order, not an express one

    No driver ends up with too much in one slot.

Rules the platform had to keep

From the consolidated requirements. They are why the domain is hard, and they are written down to the formula and the API call.

  • A sale cannot outrun the state ledger

    When
    A cart is ready at the register
    If
    Every quantity is within what the ledger allows
    Then
    Checkout opens
    Else
    The sale waits. An item can also turn unsellable in mid-shift, when the state reports a failed lab test or a hold.

  • Tax in a fixed order

    local = price × local rateexcise = (price + local) × excise ratesales = (price + local + excise) × sales rate

    Three exemption cases: a doctor's recommendation removes the local tax, a state medical card removes local and sales tax, and non-cannabis goods pay sales tax only.

  • Purchase limits follow the customer

    When
    The customer on a sale changes
    Then
    Limits are recalculated at once, in separate buckets for adult-use and medical customers, with edible weights converted from milligrams

  • Count blind, then correct the ledger

    baseline = what Metrc reports, read once at the startdifference = counted − reported

    Counters do not see the expected quantities. When a manager approves, the correction is sent to Metrc so the ledger matches the shelf.

  • What rides in a vehicle is on the delivery ledger

    When
    A driver leaves with a kit
    Then
    The kit is locked as the Metrc delivery ledger

    A restock in the field means going back to the hub and adding the packages to the active trip. The ledger is closed at the end of the shift.

  • Sales reach the state after a network drop

    When
    A sale completes
    Then
    Its receipt joins a local queue that a background worker keeps pushing to Metrc

    Checkout never waits for the state system.

Keeping the scope honest. After the client review I reissued the backlog as a delta. Every change carries a tag (Added, Changed, Removed or Confirmed) and the meeting decision or feedback point behind it, with scope split between the first release and a later version, so the team could see what moved and why without rereading everything.

Once design was under way I built a second check with Claude Code. For each designed section, an agent compared the Figma export with the original scope and listed what had grown, one row per body of engineering effort, largest first. The prompt told the agent which source wins when they disagree: the design, then the latest criteria, then the original scope. I ran it over 46 sections. Three rows from the zone-management sheet, shortened:

In the original scopeWhat the design added
Two ways to draw a zone, in one lineA full zone editor on a map, with live area and radius feedback
One delivery fee and minimum for every zoneFees and thresholds per zone, checked against each other
An optional link between a zone and one driver or shopEach zone bound to a shop and to any number of drivers, with a daily order cap

What moved to later. In the reissued backlog, in-app route optimization for multi-stop routes moves from the first release to the later version.

None of this is AI in the product: the automation is deterministic rules, third-party APIs and one OCR feature for reading documents.

06

Outcome

The client praised the discovery to Valere’s leadership and liked the delivery design. Development had started, and I was running sprint planning, reviews and refinement and coordinating design and engineering, when my work on the project ended in July 2026.

07

Learnings

The rule that reshaped the driver’s kit surfaced almost last. Everything loaded onto a vehicle has to sit on a delivery ledger in Metrc, and that was stated in a PDF on the state’s website, where it was easy to miss. With the technical PM I worked it into the requirements: the kit is locked as that ledger at departure, nothing joins it in the field, and it is closed at the end of the shift.

On a platform this size my job is to trace how one change touches every other part. This one I traced late.
