01

Problem

A medical-aesthetics app was meant to analyze a user’s selfie, suggest treatments and route the user to a consultation at a partner clinic. A suggestion names a procedure, but it does not show the person what that procedure might change.

I led the discovery at Valere, working with the client on the product and its AI scope.

02

Discovery

As the discovery scoped it, the product had three parts: a mobile app where people set wellness and aesthetic goals, send daily check-ins, wearable data and a selfie, and receive recommendations; a web app where clinicians confirm consultations and run treatment plans; and an admin portal for pilot metrics across two partner clinics.

On the aesthetics side, the client’s brief stopped at treatment suggestions. Other apps in the market gave me the idea for this one: show the person, not only the procedure.

03

Key decision

The call
Propose generative before-and-after imagery alongside the treatment suggestions the client asked for.
Alternatives
Keep the aesthetics side to suggesting treatments.
Why
A suggestion names a procedure; an image of the person's own face shows what it might change. I researched market examples and cost before proposing it, and the client approved the concept.
04

Approach

  • Go beyond the ask. I proposed generative before-and-after imagery: next to a suggested treatment, an image of the user’s own face showing a plausible result.
  • Make it costable before selling it. I researched market examples and their cost, and recommended a model on that basis, so the client could weigh the idea and its price together.
  • Hand over something buildable. I supplied prompt examples and model suggestions, and set the expectation that the client would provide the training data.
  • Build restraint into the experience. In the specification, the user picks one of three intensity levels (natural, non-invasive, surgical), compares before and after with a slider, and sees a mandatory legal disclaimer on the surgical simulation. A selfie older than 30 days triggers a prompt for a new one before a treatment.

The client approved the concept.

05

AI design choices

As specified, generation sits at the end of a chain, and each step is bounded.

  • Two models, two jobs. An analytical computer-vision model, trained on expert-labelled facial images, finds areas of focus on the face and reports only those above a 0.70 confidence threshold. A generative model, trained on before-and-after photo pairs, then renders the preview.
  • What the user sees. A facial analysis report, the preview at the chosen intensity and a comparison slider, each leading to a consultation request.
  • How quality is judged. The generative model is held to “Facetune-level realism” and told “not to show exaggerated effects”: a preview that oversells counts as a failure, however good it looks. Generated progress text passes content moderation before it is shown.
  • The clinician in the loop. The clinician’s official before-photo overrides the user’s selfie as the source of truth, and AI-suggested check-in questions are edited by the clinician and then locked, so every milestone is measured the same way.
  • Rules recommend, not the model. The wellness side runs a separate, non-generative classifier at a 0.75 threshold; rules map its flags to clinic services, ranked 0.7 on model relevance and 0.3 on the user’s own goals.
06

Outcome

The client approved the generative-preview concept and praised the work to Valere leadership. The build was quoted above the client’s budget, so the engagement closed at the approved concept and the costed proposal.

What the discovery left is a specification in which the AI has a clear job and clear limits: a diagnostic model that must be confident, a generative model that must be modest, and a clinician who holds the record.
